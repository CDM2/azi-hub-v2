Here is the Corpus Blind Spot Audit. This analysis strictly evaluates the provided materials to identify technical, strategic, and cognitive gaps in the current research foundation. In accordance with your instructions, no external product recommendations or architectural conclusions are made. Instead, these gaps are framed as hypotheses that demand validation.

1. CORPUS BLIND SPOTS

The current corpus heavily indexes on hierarchical enterprise structures and rigid, top-down governance. It currently lacks knowledge or operational assumptions across the following dimensions:

  1. Missing technical categories: The corpus overlooks event-driven agent architectures, stream-processing for real-time enterprise data ingestion, and distributed coordination methodologies that do not rely on a centralized orchestration layer.
  2. Missing Agentic OS architectures: There is no exploration of decentralized peer-to-peer agent meshes, edge-deployed autonomous agents, or serverless agent execution environments.
  3. Missing research paradigms: The foundation ignores established Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) academic literature, human-computer interaction (HCI) research on human-AI teaming, and complex adaptive systems theory.
  4. Missing competitors / adjacent systems: The analysis omits open-source multi-agent frameworks (e.g., AutoGen, LangGraph), iPaaS incumbents (e.g., MuleSoft, Zapier) advancing toward cognitive orchestration, and next-generation RPA vendors evolving beyond static workflows.
  5. Missing academic/research traditions: There is no reference to actor-model programming semantics or organizational behavior psychology concerning algorithmic management and trust.
  6. Missing business models: The corpus does not evaluate outcome-based pricing, consumption-based pricing beyond token pass-throughs, or hybrid models that monetize strategic implementation services.
  7. Missing customer categories: The plan misses non-regulated knowledge workers, gig economy/freelancer networks, physical operations (manufacturing/logistics), and non-profit or government sectors.
  8. Missing economic considerations: The corpus acknowledges but does not resolve the computational cost of continuous evaluation and Knowledge Graph maintenance. It also ignores the economic threshold where human-in-the-loop approvals become more expensive than the automated task itself.
  9. Missing moat mechanisms: There is no investigation into network effects generated by cross-company playbook sharing, regulatory compliance certifications as a barrier to entry, or the proprietary data exhaust derived from capturing human corrections over time.
  10. Missing failure modes: The plan lacks assumptions around cascading systemic failures across interdependent agents, large-scale prompt injection, and the specific failure rate at which human-in-the-loop approval degrades into notification fatigue.
  11. Missing assumptions about self-optimization: The research does not address the risk of catastrophic forgetting, over-fitting to narrow evaluation metrics, or the possibility that underlying foundation models plateau in their reasoning capabilities.
  12. Missing assumptions about orchestration: The distinction between centralized orchestration and decentralized choreography is absent. It also lacks mechanisms for resolving conflicts when agents compete for limited enterprise resources or API rate limits.
  13. Missing assumptions about memory/knowledge: The corpus misses strategic forgetting mechanisms (defining when data should be intentionally purged) and ways to resolve semantic ambiguity without explicitly dictating contradictory facts between Layer 1 and Layer 2 memory.
  14. Missing assumptions about organizational structure: There is no consideration for dynamic, ephemeral cross-functional agent teams that form and dissolve based on tasks, contrasting heavily with the assumption that the system maps to a static corporate hierarchy.
  15. Missing assumptions about governance: The corpus overlooks cross-border data sovereignty, comprehensive compliance frameworks, and the legal liability of autonomous agent actions.
  16. Missing assumptions about build-vs-buy: There is a blind spot regarding hyperscalers (AWS, Azure, GCP) potentially commoditizing the orchestration framework and rendering custom control planes redundant.
  17. Missing assumptions about model commoditization: The corpus assumes a persistent need for custom memory and complex self-improvement loops. It fails to consider that off-the-shelf LLMs may natively incorporate large-context reasoning, tool use, and long-term memory at the base-model layer in the near future.
  18. Missing assumptions about incumbent platform behavior: The analysis ignores the risk of major CRM/ERP incumbents utilizing their immense distribution advantages and system-of-record lock-in to natively provide "good enough" orchestration.

2. POTENTIAL MISSING RESEARCH DOMAINS

The explicit distinction between Greenfield and Brownfield transformation must become a primary research vector.

• The Greenfield vs. Brownfield Dichotomy: The current plan sets an initial boundary to integrate with systems of record. However, it lacks a structured investigation into whether legacy-to-AI-native transformation (brownfield) requires a fundamentally different architecture and operating model than autonomous venture creation (greenfield).
• Legacy Ingestion and Discovery: A critical missing domain is how an AI system can safely and dynamically discover, map, and ingest unstructured institutional knowledge, complex RBAC permissions, and undocumented shadow IT without tedious manual configuration.
• Transformation Modalities: Research must determine if brownfield transformation is better positioned as a standalone software product, an implementation/consulting service, or a hybrid motion.
• Architecture of Augmentation: Investigating the architectural divergence between acting as a centralized "Enterprise Brain" versus operating as a progressive integration mesh that seamlessly wraps legacy APIs.

3. POTENTIAL MISSING COMPETITIVE/TECHNICAL APPROACHES

• Decentralized Orchestration Fabrics: Exploring peer-to-peer agent coordination architectures instead of relying solely on a top-down dynamic context "Conductor Engine".
• Progressive System Wrapping: Utilizing agents to wrap non-idempotent legacy APIs with natural language interfaces, providing a modernization layer without requiring immediate systemic replacement or migration.
• Enterprise-Grade Onboarding Mechanisms: Addressing the rigorous technical approaches required for secure enterprise embedding, including Virtual Private Cloud (VPC) deployments, on-premise bridging, SOC 2 compliance frameworks, and strict data isolation.

4. POTENTIAL MISSING CUSTOMER/ECONOMIC QUESTIONS

• The Go-to-Market Wedge: Could brownfield legacy transformation serve as the most lucrative initial commercial wedge, with greenfield company creation reserved for later expansion once market trust is established?
• Onboarding as a Proprietary Engine: Can secure enterprise onboarding and the transformation process itself generate the proprietary data exhaust and evaluation loop needed to build CAT's moat?
• Knowledge Reusability: Does the successful transformation of an existing company create reusable organizational schemas that accelerate CAT's ability to transform subsequent customers?
• Margin Dilution: If onboarding legacy organizations requires intensive human-in-the-loop implementation specialists, does this shift the business model toward consulting and dilute SaaS margins?

5. POTENTIAL COGNITIVE BIASES IN THE CURRENT CORPUS

• The Founder-Build (AZI) Bias: The assumption that specific features designed for a solo founder's internal tool—such as ephemeral agents, explicit "Alphabet layer" hierarchies, and multi-layered hybrid memory—are universally required for an external commercial product.
• The Top-Down Enterprise (McKinsey) Bias: Over-indexing on massive enterprise change management frameworks. This leads to the unvalidated assumption that the product requires a monolithic "Enterprise Brain" or a concentric graph capable of rendering up to 50,000 corporate nodes.
• The Skeptical Reset Bias: The CAT methodology's rigid insistence on testing against single-agent, deterministic baselines. This strict methodological filtering might artificially stifle the exploration of emergent multi-agent capabilities that could unlock unique value.
• The Systems of Record Bias: The unquestioned mandate that CAT must integrate with existing systems of record rather than replace them. This blinds the research to the possibility that truly AI-native organizations may abandon traditional ERP/CRM systems entirely.

6. QUESTIONS THAT SHOULD BE ADDED BEFORE THE LANDSCAPE RESEARCH

• Does onboarding an existing enterprise (brownfield) require a fundamentally different architecture and operating model than creating a new venture (greenfield)?
• How would CAT securely operate across legacy systems without requiring immediate replacement, and what specific deployment constraints (e.g., VPC, SOC 2, secrets management) arise when embedded in an existing enterprise?
• Does the brownfield use case make the "organizational operating system" thesis more compelling, or does it imply that CAT merely needs an integration/control plane rather than a true OS?
• Could legacy transformation serve as the initial commercial wedge, or is greenfield venture creation strategically superior?
• What exact elements (workflows, data, permissions, institutional knowledge) must CAT be able to map from a legacy organization to establish a safe operating baseline?
• What is the ongoing role of human consultants during onboarding, and could that hybrid motion become a competitive advantage?

7. QUESTIONS THAT SHOULD BE REMOVED OR DEPRIORITIZED BECAUSE THEY ARE TOO AZI-SPECIFIC

• "Are the evaluation datasets (currently 10-20 items) sufficient for genuine self-improvement signal?" This is prematurely focused on the historical implementation's limitations rather than general market requirements.
• "How do we prevent the recursive self-critique (SCOPE) loop from overfitting to its own grader biases?" This over-indexes on an advanced, unvalidated feature when the basic commercial wedge remains entirely unproven.
• Questions centered strictly on validating the "Alphabet layer" (meta-org and subsidiaries). This architecture may not apply to target buyers seeking simple workflow automation.
• Granular debates regarding "Layer 1 Raw vs Layer 2 Compiled Wiki" memory. Focusing on the specifics of this hybrid storage engine distracts from whether complex persistent state is even necessary for the initial product boundary.

Follow-up: Based on these identified blind spots, should the initial customer hypotheses (Phase 2A) be formally split to isolate greenfield venture studios from legacy SMB/enterprise operations, allowing us to test them as entirely distinct products?

8. ADDITIONAL RESEARCH DIMENSION — SINGLE ORGANIZATION VS. MULTI-ORGANIZATION

In addition to the Greenfield vs. Brownfield distinction identified above, the research program should explicitly investigate a second strategic dimension: whether CAT is ultimately valuable within a single organization or across multiple organizations.

This should remain a hypothesis space rather than a product conclusion.

The resulting 2x2 framework is:

|          |Single Organization                                               |Multi-Organization                                                               |
|----------|------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Greenfield|Create/run one new AI-native organization                         |Create/run a portfolio of new AI-native organizations                            |
|Brownfield|Transform one existing organization into an AI-native organization|Transform/manage a portfolio of existing organizations toward AI-native operation|

The research should determine whether these represent:

• different products,
• different customer segments,
• different go-to-market motions,
• different stages of one product,
• different applications of a common underlying control plane,
• or fundamentally different businesses.

The research should explicitly investigate the Brownfield + Multi-Organization quadrant as well as the other three quadrants.

Examples of hypotheses to investigate include:

• A PE/VC firm or holding company could potentially use CAT across a portfolio of existing companies.
• A platform could potentially create and manage multiple greenfield ventures.
• A single existing enterprise could use CAT to progressively transform its operating model without immediately replacing its systems of record.
• A single new venture could use CAT to determine and provision the organizational capabilities it actually needs.

None of these should be treated as validated customer, product, or architecture conclusions.

The research should determine what evidence would be required to compare the four quadrants across:

• customer pain and willingness to pay
• time to value
• implementation complexity
• technical requirements
• security and governance requirements
• recurring revenue potential
• gross margin potential
• defensibility / moat
• expansion potential
• data and learning loops
• dependence on third-party models/platforms
• ability to become a durable control point

This additional dimension should be incorporated into subsequent research planning rather than treated as a modification to the original corpus conclusions.
